If you are buying an air purifier because of **smoke or smells**, do not assume that every purifier tackles both problems equally well.

The useful distinction is between **particles** and **gases/odours**.

## HEPA and carbon do different jobs

A particle filter such as HEPA is primarily about airborne particles. Activated carbon is the part of many purifier systems intended to adsorb some gases and odours.

That means a purifier advertised around HEPA alone should not automatically be treated as an odour-removal machine.

## For smoke

Smoke can contain both particles and gaseous compounds. A purifier therefore needs meaningful particle filtration, while carbon capacity becomes relevant for odours and some gaseous pollutants.

The amount of carbon matters. A token carbon sheet and a substantial carbon filter are not necessarily comparable simply because both product pages use the word 'carbon'.

## For cooking smells

Start with extraction and ventilation. A cooker hood vented outside, where available and used correctly, tackles pollution closer to its source.

A purifier can be an additional layer, but it should not become an excuse to ignore source control.

## For tobacco or persistent indoor smoke

Removing or reducing the source is much more important than expecting a purifier to make ongoing indoor smoking harmless. Filtration can reduce some airborne pollution; it cannot make a heavily polluted environment equivalent to clean air.

## Check room size and useful-speed noise

A purifier that moves enough air only on an intolerably loud maximum setting may be a poor everyday choice. Compare room-size guidance with the speed you are realistically willing to run.
