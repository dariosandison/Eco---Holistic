A short disruption does not require a cupboard full of specialist survival meals.

For most households, a better approach is to keep **three days of food you already eat**, chosen so that at least some of it can be eaten with little or no cooking.

The aim is simple: if shops are closed, card systems are unavailable, transport is disrupted or the power is off for a while, you can still feed the household without immediately needing to improvise.

## Start with normal food

The easiest emergency food is familiar food with a longer shelf life.

Useful categories include:

- tinned fish, meat, beans and pulses;
- nut butters;
- oatcakes, crackers or crispbreads;
- oats and simple cereals;
- dried fruit and nuts;
- shelf-stable milk or suitable alternatives;
- tins or jars of fruit and vegetables;
- ready-to-eat meals that suit your household;
- baby food, pet food and specialist dietary products where required.

Rotate these through normal meals rather than building a forgotten emergency cupboard.

## Plan for no cooking

A power cut can remove the hob, oven, microwave and kettle at the same time.

Make sure part of your three-day food buffer can be eaten safely without heating. That could be as simple as tinned fish, nut butter, crackers, fruit, shelf-stable milk and ready-to-eat foods.

If you store canned food, keep a **manual can opener** with it.

## Water matters more than food variety

Dry foods can increase water needs, and some meals require water to prepare.

Plan food and water together. Our [72-hour household water plan](/blog/72-hour-household-water-plan-uk) explains the drinking-water side in more detail.

## Do not rely on the freezer

A full freezer can stay cold for a while if it remains closed, but the exact safe time depends on the appliance, room temperature and how often the door is opened.

Treat frozen food as a bonus, not your only emergency food supply.

If a prolonged power cut occurs, follow current food-safety advice rather than guessing whether food is safe because it still feels cool.

## Build the buffer gradually

You do not need a separate £200 shopping trip.

Add one or two useful items to normal shopping until you have a comfortable three-day buffer, then rotate them through ordinary meals.

This keeps cost down and avoids waste.

## Household-specific planning

A generic list is not enough for every home. Consider:

- babies and young children;
- allergies and intolerances;
- diabetes or other dietary requirements;
- elderly household members;
- pets;
- prescription nutrition products;
- anyone who struggles to chew or swallow certain foods.

## A simple three-day framework

Rather than planning nine elaborate meals, think in categories:

**Protein:** tinned fish, meat, beans, pulses, nut butter.

**Carbohydrate:** oats, crackers, cereal, wraps or other shelf-stable staples.

**Fruit and vegetables:** tins, jars, dried fruit and fresh produce that keeps well.

**Fluids:** stored drinking water first; shelf-stable milk can add variety but does not replace water.

**Comfort:** tea, coffee or a small familiar snack can make an inconvenient few days feel much more normal.

## What not to buy first

Specialist freeze-dried meals, huge sacks of food and expensive long-life kits are rarely the best first step for an ordinary household.

Before buying something specifically for emergencies, ask whether your household would actually eat it, whether it needs cooking, and whether you can rotate it through normal use.

## The Wild & Well rule

**Store what you eat, and eat what you store.**

A small rotating buffer of normal food is cheaper, easier to maintain and more useful than a cupboard of unfamiliar products bought out of fear.
