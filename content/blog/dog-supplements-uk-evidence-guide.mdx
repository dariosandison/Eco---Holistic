Dog supplements are easy to buy and difficult to assess. A familiar ingredient, thousands of reviews and a veterinary-looking pack do not prove that the finished product will produce a meaningful benefit for your dog.

The safest starting point is a more specific question: **what problem are you trying to solve, and does that problem need a vet before it needs a supplement?**

## Start with the dog, not the ingredient

Speak to a vet before adding a supplement when there is:

- pain, stiffness, lameness or reduced movement;
- persistent itching, hair loss or inflamed skin;
- ongoing diarrhoea, vomiting or appetite change;
- unexplained weight loss or gain;
- medication, a diagnosed condition or a therapeutic diet;
- pregnancy, growth or complex nutritional needs.

These signs can have several causes. Masking them with a wellness chew can delay useful assessment.

## Five questions for any supplement

| Question | Stronger answer | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| What is the intended use? | A narrow, measurable purpose | “Total health”, detox or cure-all language |
| What is in a daily amount? | Exact named ingredients and quantities | A proprietary blend with no useful amounts |
| Is evidence product-specific? | Research on the formulation or a closely matched dose | General research on one ingredient |
| Who should avoid it? | Clear cautions and contact details | “Completely safe because it is natural” |
| How will you judge it? | A defined trial period and outcome | Indefinite use based on vague reassurance |

## Ingredient evidence is not product evidence

A study of an ingredient does not automatically validate every chew containing it. The form, dose, combination, manufacturing quality and population studied may all differ.

When we cover a supplement, we separate:

- **established veterinary guidance**;
- **evidence for the ingredient**;
- **evidence for the finished product and daily amount**;
- **manufacturer claims**;
- **our judgement about transparency, cost and usability**.

## Joint, digestive and skin products

These are commercially popular categories, but each needs restraint:

### Joints and mobility

Stiffness or reluctance to move deserves veterinary assessment. Weight management, appropriate activity, pain management and home adaptations may matter more than adding a supplement.

### Digestion

“Probiotic” does not describe one interchangeable ingredient. Strain, amount, storage and the condition being studied matter. Persistent digestive signs need investigation.

### Skin and coat

Itching may involve parasites, infection, environmental allergy, food allergy or other causes. Oil or skin chews should not be used to guess the diagnosis.

## Avoid stacking

Starting several products at once makes benefits, side effects and cost harder to interpret. It can also duplicate nutrients.

If a vet agrees a supplement is reasonable:

1. choose one product for one defined purpose;
2. record the exact product and daily amount;
3. agree what improvement would count;
4. review it after a defined period;
5. stop or seek advice if unwanted effects appear.
