Winter indoor-air problems often get lumped together, but **dry air, condensation and damp need different responses**. The useful first move is not buying a machine — it is measuring what is actually happening.

> **Quick routes:** [Air quality topic →](/topics/air-quality) • [Dehumidifier guide →](/best-dehumidifiers-damp-mould-uk) • [Air quality shortlist →](/air-quality-shortlist-uk)

## Start by measuring for a few days

Put a simple hygrometer in the room where the problem is most obvious, ideally away from radiators, windows and direct steam.

You are looking for a pattern, not one isolated reading.

As a practical comfort guide:
- **very low readings for sustained periods** can point toward overly dry indoor air
- **persistent readings above roughly 60% RH** make condensation and mould more likely, especially around cold surfaces
- readings in the middle are often more comfortable, but the building, temperature and moisture sources still matter

## What should you set a dehumidifier to in winter?

For an ordinary heated UK room, **55% RH is a sensible starting setpoint**, not a universal medical or building-safety threshold. Watch what happens for several days rather than chasing one exact number:

- if windows and cold corners stay dry and the machine cycles off, keep the setting where it is;
- if measured humidity remains above 60% for long periods, check extraction, indoor laundry, leaks and cold surfaces before simply selecting a lower number;
- if the room feels excessively dry or the machine runs almost continuously, move the setting towards 60% and reassess;
- measure away from the machine, radiator and window because the display beside the air intake may not represent the whole room.

Several UK councils describe **40–60% RH** as a useful household range. That is guidance rather than proof that every room must sit at the same number. Condensation depends on surface temperature as well as room humidity, so a cold window or uninsulated wall can still become wet when the room reading looks reasonable.

Government damp-and-mould guidance also says the response should match the dwelling: ventilation, adequate heating, energy efficiency and building defects can matter alongside occupant moisture. A dehumidifier can support that plan, but it should not be used to disguise a leak, failed extractor or landlord repair issue.

## Which problem do you actually have?

| What you notice | More likely issue | First response |
|---|---|---|
| Dry throat, static, very low RH | Dry indoor air | Reduce over-heating, measure consistently, consider humidification only if readings stay low |
| Wet windows after showers or cooking | Moisture + cold surfaces | Improve extraction and short bursts of ventilation |
| Musty smell, recurring condensation, RH staying high | Excess moisture / damp risk | Find the moisture source, improve ventilation, then compare dehumidifiers if needed |
| Dust, pollen or smoke particles with normal humidity | Particle pollution | Consider filtration rather than humidity equipment |

## If the air is too dry

Do not automatically add a humidifier because the room *feels* dry. Check the readings first.

Useful steps include:
- avoid overheating the room
- check whether a heater is running for long periods
- ventilate normally rather than aggressively drying the room
- if RH remains very low for days, then consider a humidifier sized for the room

If you use a humidifier, clean it exactly as the manufacturer specifies. Poorly maintained humidifiers can introduce their own problems.

## If windows are wet in the morning

Condensation usually means warm moist indoor air is meeting a cold surface. Focus first on the moisture source.

Common winter contributors are:
- showers
- cooking
- drying laundry indoors
- bedrooms with closed windows and doors overnight
- furniture tight against cold external walls

Use extractor fans during moisture-heavy activities and for a period afterwards, then use short, purposeful ventilation rather than leaving windows slightly open all day.

## If humidity stays high

A dehumidifier can be useful when moisture remains high despite sensible ventilation, especially where indoor laundry drying is unavoidable or condensation keeps returning.

Before buying one, decide:
- which room it will serve
- whether the room is warm or cold
- whether laundry drying is a major use case
- acceptable noise level
- whether continuous drainage is useful
- likely running cost

## If humidity is normal but the air still feels poor

A dehumidifier is not the answer to every indoor-air complaint.

If the main problem is:
- dust
- pollen
- smoke particles
- traffic pollution entering the home

then filtration may be the more relevant route. Start with [Healthy Air at Home →](/blog/healthy-air-at-home) or compare the [Air quality shortlist →](/air-quality-shortlist-uk).

## Winter room-by-room checklist

### Bathroom
Run the extractor during showers and afterwards. Keep steam from spreading through the home where possible.

### Kitchen
Use pan lids and extraction while cooking. Vent briefly after high-steam cooking.

### Bedroom
Check morning humidity and condensation. Bedrooms can accumulate moisture overnight even when the rest of the home seems fine.

### Laundry area
If clothes must dry indoors, keep the process contained and ventilated. Persistent indoor laundry is one of the stronger reasons to consider a dehumidifier.

## The purchase decision

Buy a **humidifier** when measured humidity is consistently too low and simple changes have not fixed it.

Buy a **dehumidifier** when measured humidity is consistently high, moisture sources are understood, and ventilation alone is not enough.

Buy an **air purifier** when particle pollution is the problem and humidity is already under control.
