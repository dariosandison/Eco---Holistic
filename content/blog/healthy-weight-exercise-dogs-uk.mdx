Healthy weight is not a single number shared by every dog of a breed. Build, age, muscle, health and body condition all matter. A useful routine combines **regular body-condition checks, measured food and activity suited to the individual dog**.

## Check shape as well as weight

[PDSA’s healthy-weight guidance](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/puppies-dogs/keeping-your-dog-s-weight-in-check) describes looking from above and from the side and gently feeling the ribs, spine, hips and tail base.

You are looking for trends, not trying to diagnose at home. Ask your veterinary practice to show you how to body-condition score your dog and record a baseline.

Contact a vet about rapid or unexplained weight change, altered thirst or appetite, low energy, vomiting, diarrhoea or difficulty exercising.

## Count treats as food

A complete diet can still lead to weight gain if the total daily amount is too high. Measure the main food and account for treats, chews, scraps and food used in training.

Practical options include:

- reserving part of the measured daily ration for training;
- breaking suitable treats into smaller pieces;
- using praise, play or access to a favourite activity as rewards;
- involving the whole household so food is not duplicated.

Do not make a severe food cut without advice. [PDSA’s obesity guidance](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/pet-health-hub/conditions/obesity-in-dogs) recommends veterinary or vet-nurse help with an appropriate weight-reduction plan.

## Exercise needs are individual

There is no responsible universal minutes-per-day target for every dog. [PDSA’s exercise guide](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/puppies-dogs/how-much-exercise-does-your-dog-need) highlights health, age, breed and the individual dog.

A balanced week can include:

- regular walks at an appropriate pace;
- sniffing and exploration;
- reward-based training;
- safe play;
- rest and recovery;
- gradual progression rather than sudden weekend overload.

Puppies, older dogs, overweight dogs and dogs with joint or health problems need particular care with impact and sudden changes.

## Tracking can help, but it is not a diagnosis

A dog activity monitor may help a household notice routine changes, compare typical weeks and keep different carers consistent. It cannot tell you why activity changed.

Use trends such as:

- usual active time;
- rest pattern;
- walking consistency;
- weight and body-condition checks;
- behaviour or willingness to move.

If activity falls without an obvious reason, or movement appears painful, contact your vet rather than increasing a target.

## A four-week baseline

### Week 1: observe

Record the current food, treats, walks, play and weight/body condition without trying to perfect everything.

### Week 2: remove measurement errors

Use the same scoop or, preferably, weigh food. Agree the treat plan across the household.

### Week 3: stabilise movement

Aim for a repeatable pattern suited to the dog. Avoid using one very long outing to compensate for an inactive week.

### Week 4: review

Compare energy, appetite, stools, movement, body condition and the practicality of the routine. Take concerns to the veterinary team.

## Before buying anything

Food scales, a written household plan and consistent walks may be more useful than a supplement or tracker. A product earns its place only when it makes the established routine easier to follow or provides genuinely useful information.
