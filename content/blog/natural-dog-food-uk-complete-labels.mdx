“Natural” can describe part of a dog food, but it does not tell you whether the food is nutritionally complete, appropriate for your dog or supported by better evidence than a less rustic-looking alternative.

For an everyday food, the most useful first word on a UK label is usually **complete**.

## Complete and complementary are different jobs

[UK Pet Food’s labelling guidance](https://www.ukpetfood.org/resource/labelling-factsheet.html) explains that a label should state whether a product is complete or complementary, which species it is intended for and how it should be fed.

- **Complete food** is formulated to provide the nutrients required for the stated life stage when fed as directed.
- **Complementary food** is not designed to provide the whole diet by itself. Treats, toppers and many supplements fall into this category.

That does not make every complete food identical or every complementary product poor. It tells you the job the product is legally intended to do.

## A calm five-point label check

| Check | What to find | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Legal description | Complete or complementary | Establishes whether it can form the whole diet |
| Intended dog | Puppy, adult, senior or a specific purpose | Nutritional needs change with life stage |
| Feeding directions | Daily amount and how it is calculated | Portions affect weight and nutrient intake |
| Composition | Ingredients listed in descending weight order | Useful context, but not a nutrition score by itself |
| Analytical constituents | Protein, fat, fibre, ash and moisture declarations | Helps comparison only when products are compared properly |

Wet and dry foods contain very different amounts of water. Comparing the protein percentages on the pack without accounting for moisture can be misleading.

## What does “natural” establish?

It may describe how particular ingredients have been handled or positioned, but it does not establish:

- that the food is complete;
- that it is appropriate for a medical condition;
- that every ingredient has a meaningful benefit;
- that the formulation has been tested more thoroughly;
- that it will suit an individual dog.

The same caution applies to words such as *premium*, *holistic*, *ancestral* and *superfood*. Treat them as prompts to inspect the actual formulation and evidence, not conclusions.

## Ingredients are not the whole formulation

An attractive ingredient list can still leave important questions unanswered. A manufacturer should be able to explain:

- who formulates the diet;
- which nutritional standard it follows;
- how quality and safety are controlled;
- whether it is complete for the stated life stage;
- how feeding amounts were established;
- how to contact the company with a nutrition question.

Do not assume a long list is automatically worse, or that a short list is automatically balanced.

## Raw feeding needs an extra safety check

Raw food can be commercially complete or complementary, so check the label rather than assuming. It also requires careful storage, handling and household hygiene. [UK Pet Food’s responsible raw-feeding guidance](https://www.ukpetfood.org/asset/5416C818-C55D-492C-A6F68B1B6C4B1651/) emphasises checking the complete/complementary description and following handling directions.

Households with young children, older people, pregnancy or weakened immunity should discuss food-safety implications with a vet or appropriate health professional.

## Change food gradually

Abrupt changes can upset digestion. Follow the new food’s transition instructions unless your vet has given a different plan. Track appetite, stools, vomiting, itching and energy, but do not try to diagnose a reaction from a single sign.

Contact a vet about persistent digestive problems, suspected food allergy, unexplained weight change or a dog needing a therapeutic diet. An elimination diet for suspected allergy should be properly designed rather than improvised by repeatedly switching premium foods.

## The Wild & Well decision rule

1. Check that the main diet is complete and appropriate for the dog’s life stage.
2. Measure portions and include treats in the daily intake.
3. Treat “natural” and ingredient-led claims as questions, not proof.
4. Ask the manufacturer about formulation and quality controls.
5. Involve a vet when symptoms, weight change or a clinical diet are involved.
