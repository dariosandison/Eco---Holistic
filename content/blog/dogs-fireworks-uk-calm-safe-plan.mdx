Fireworks can be difficult for dogs because the noise, flashes and timing are unpredictable. The useful response is not to buy a cupboard of calming products. It is to make the home safer, reduce avoidable exposure and involve your vet early if your dog has previously shown significant fear.

This is general household guidance, not a diagnosis or an individual treatment plan.

## The short version

If fireworks may start tonight:

1. Walk and toilet your dog **before dark**, while keeping the normal routine as steady as possible.
2. Keep doors, windows, gates and garden access secure.
3. Prepare a familiar hiding or resting place and let your dog choose whether to use it.
4. Close curtains and use familiar background sound to soften sudden noise and flashes.
5. Stay calm and available. Do not punish fearful behaviour or force your dog out of hiding.
6. Contact your vet if distress is severe, persistent or has happened before.

[Dogs Trust](https://www.dogstrust.org.uk/dog-advice/life-with-your-dog/seasonal/fireworks), [RSPCA](https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/fireworks/pets) and [PDSA](https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/pet-health-hub/other-veterinary-advice/dogs-and-fireworks) all publish UK fireworks guidance for pet owners.

## Prepare before the first bang

### Check the household boundary

A startled dog may try to bolt. Before dusk, check:

- external doors and windows;
- garden gates and damaged fencing;
- cat flaps or other exits a dog could reach;
- collar and ID-tag details;
- microchip contact details.

Do not leave a dog outside alone during likely fireworks. PDSA advises keeping dogs supervised and secure because startled dogs can escape from gardens.

### Build a safe place, not a trap

Choose a quiet, familiar area away from the busiest entrance and largest windows. Add the dog's usual bedding, water and familiar items. A crate is appropriate only if the dog is already comfortable using it; suddenly confining a frightened dog in an unfamiliar crate can add stress.

Let the dog enter and leave the space. Do not drag them out for reassurance or insist that they stay there.

### Plan walks for daylight

Move the main walk earlier rather than risking a busy outing after dark. Keep dogs on a secure lead during fireworks season, even if their recall is usually reliable. A sudden bang can produce behaviour that is very different from an ordinary walk.

If your dog needs a late toilet break, keep it brief, supervised and close to home.

## What to do while fireworks are going off

Close windows and curtains before the noise begins. Familiar television, radio, a fan or steady music may help mask some external sound, but keep the volume comfortable rather than trying to overpower fireworks.

Offer normal company and allow your dog to choose proximity. It is fine to comfort a dog that seeks you out. Equally, do not crowd a dog that wants to hide.

Useful low-pressure options include:

- a familiar chew or food toy, if the dog is willing to eat;
- calm indoor sniffing or searching games;
- access to the prepared safe space;
- keeping children and visitors from disturbing the dog;
- maintaining a steady room temperature and access to water.

If the dog refuses food, do not keep presenting increasingly rich treats. Loss of appetite can be part of fear, and the priority is safety and calm rather than completing an activity.

## Signs your dog may be struggling

Fear is not always loud. Look for changes such as:

- panting, trembling or pacing;
- repeated hiding or attempts to escape;
- drooling, toileting or refusing food;
- unusually fixed attention, scanning or startling;
- destructive behaviour around doors or windows;
- inability to settle after the noise has stopped.

Contact your vet promptly if your dog is at risk of injury, has severe or escalating distress, collapses, has breathing difficulty, or behaves in a way that makes safe handling difficult. For a dog with a known fireworks problem, speak to the veterinary practice **before** the main fireworks period rather than waiting for the loudest night.

## Calming products: where evidence and marketing separate

Pheromone products, supplements, pressure garments and calming treats are widely marketed. They are not interchangeable, and a product labelled “natural” is not automatically effective or appropriate.

Before buying, ask:

- What exact problem is this meant to address?
- Is the evidence about this finished product, or only one ingredient?
- Is the dose or intended use clear?
- Could it interact with medication or an existing condition?
- Will it distract from the environmental and behavioural plan?

Do not give human medication or use a sedating product without veterinary direction. If prescription medication may be appropriate, that is a conversation for your vet, ideally with time to plan and follow their instructions.

## Sound training is long-term work

Gradual sound desensitisation and counter-conditioning can help some dogs, but the timing and intensity matter. Playing loud firework recordings immediately before Bonfire Night can make fear worse rather than prepare a dog.

[Dogs Trust's sound therapy guidance](https://www.dogstrust.org.uk/dog-advice/understanding-your-dog/sound-therapy-for-pets) is designed for gradual work outside a crisis. Dogs with substantial fear may need a vet and an appropriately qualified clinical animal behaviourist.

## A 24-hour household checklist

- [ ] Check local event dates and likely start times.
- [ ] Complete the main walk before dark.
- [ ] Check doors, windows, gates, ID tag and microchip details.
- [ ] Set up water, bedding and the familiar safe space.
- [ ] Close curtains before flashes begin.
- [ ] Prepare familiar background sound.
- [ ] Tell children and visitors not to disturb a hiding dog.
- [ ] Keep the late toilet break brief, supervised and secure.
- [ ] Save the veterinary practice and out-of-hours numbers.
- [ ] Record what helped, ready for the next event.

In most of the UK, fireworks generally cannot be used between 11pm and 7am, with later exceptions including Bonfire Night, Diwali, New Year's Eve and Chinese New Year. The [UK Government fireworks-law page](https://www.gov.uk/fireworks-the-law) explains the current cut-off times. Local displays and private fireworks may still make the exact household window unpredictable.

## After the event

Give the dog time to settle and return to the normal routine. Check the garden before the next unsupervised use for firework debris or damaged fencing. Note the time, duration, signs of fear and what the dog chose to do.

That record is more useful for a veterinary or behaviour discussion than a vague memory that the dog “hated fireworks”. It also helps the household prepare earlier next time.
