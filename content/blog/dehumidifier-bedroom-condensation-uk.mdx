Bedroom windows wet every morning? A dehumidifier can help in the right situation, but condensation is a **moisture and temperature problem first**. Buying a machine without understanding where the moisture comes from can leave you treating the symptom rather than the cause.

## Why bedroom condensation happens

Warm indoor air can hold moisture from breathing, drying clothes, showers, cooking and normal household activity. When that humid air reaches a sufficiently cold surface — often a window or external wall — water can condense.

A bedroom can therefore have condensation even when there is no plumbing leak. Equally, persistent damp patches can have causes that a dehumidifier cannot fix, such as water ingress or a building defect.

## First: measure the room

A basic hygrometer gives you a much better starting point than guessing. Look at the pattern over several days rather than one isolated reading.

Also note:

- whether windows are wet mainly after sleeping;
- whether clothes are being dried indoors;
- whether trickle vents are open and clear;
- whether the room is consistently much colder than the rest of the home;
- whether there is a local damp patch unrelated to condensation.

If you rent and suspect a building defect, document the issue rather than relying on a dehumidifier to hide it. See our **[UK damp and mould renter guide](/blog/damp-mould-uk-renters-guide)**.

## Free steps before buying a dehumidifier

Try the obvious moisture controls first:

1. Use bathroom and kitchen extraction when producing steam.
2. Keep available trickle vents open unless there is a specific reason not to.
3. Avoid drying large loads of washing in the bedroom.
4. Air the room when outdoor conditions make that practical.
5. Avoid allowing the bedroom to become dramatically colder than the rest of the home.
6. Wipe persistent window condensation while you work on the underlying moisture balance.

These measures can reduce the workload a dehumidifier would otherwise have to handle.

## When a dehumidifier makes sense

A dehumidifier becomes more useful when indoor humidity remains persistently high despite sensible ventilation and moisture control, or when you have a predictable moisture load such as indoor laundry drying.

The important buying question is not simply 'what is the best dehumidifier?' It is **how much moisture does this room or home need removed, under what conditions, and how often will the unit run?**

## Bedroom-specific things to compare

### Noise

A unit that is technically effective but too loud to use when needed may be a poor bedroom choice. Check operating noise and think about whether you intend to run it while sleeping or mainly before bedtime.

### Tank size and drainage

A small tank may mean frequent emptying during a heavy moisture load. Continuous drainage can be useful in some setups, but only where it can be installed safely and practically.

### Low-temperature performance

If the bedroom is cold, compare how the type of dehumidifier performs at the temperatures you actually experience rather than relying only on headline extraction figures.

### Running cost

Purchase price is only part of ownership. Power use, operating hours and how effectively the unit reaches your target humidity all matter.

## Dehumidifier or air purifier?

They solve different problems. A **dehumidifier removes water from the air**. An **air purifier filters airborne particles**. A purifier does not lower humidity, and a dehumidifier is not a substitute for particle filtration.

If your concern is pollen, dust or airborne particles rather than condensation, start with the **[Air Quality topic](/topics/air-quality)** instead.

## Quick decision

**Occasional wet windows, obvious ventilation issue:** fix the free causes first.

**Persistent high humidity after sensible moisture control:** a dehumidifier may be useful.

**Localised damp patch, leak or suspected building defect:** investigate the source rather than expecting a machine to repair it.
