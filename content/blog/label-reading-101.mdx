Food labels are most useful when they help you **compare similar products**. They are less useful when they turn every packet into a verdict on whether a food is “good” or “bad”.

The simplest approach is to read the label in layers: **what the food is, what it contains, then the nutrition information that matters for the choice you are making**.

## The 30-second supermarket scan

### 1. Start with the name and ingredients

The ingredients list tells you what the product is actually made from. Ingredients are listed in **descending order by weight**, so the first few ingredients make up the largest share of the product.

Allergens must be emphasised on the label. If you have an allergy or medically diagnosed intolerance, use the allergen information rather than relying on front-of-pack marketing language.

### 2. Compare like with like per 100g or 100ml

UK pre-packed foods normally provide nutrition information per **100g or 100ml**, often with an additional per-portion figure. The per-100g column is particularly useful when comparing two versions of the same type of food because the manufacturer's suggested serving sizes may differ.

Depending on the decision, look at:

- **protein** — useful when comparing foods you are choosing specifically as a protein source
- **fibre** — where it is declared, useful when comparing breads, cereals, snacks and similar foods
- **sugars** — especially when comparing similar sweetened products
- **saturated fat** — useful for comparing products within the same category
- **salt** — particularly relevant for sauces, ready meals, processed meats and savoury snacks
- **energy** — useful context, but not a complete measure of food quality

No single number tells you whether a food fits your diet.

### 3. Use front-of-pack colours as a shortcut, not the whole decision

Many UK products use red, amber and green front-of-pack nutrition labels. They give a quick indication of whether a product is high, medium or low in fat, saturated fat, sugars and salt.

They are useful for comparing similar products quickly. A red marker does not mean a food is forbidden; it means that nutrient is high and gives you context for frequency and portion size.

## Claims on the front are not the same as the nutrition panel

Words such as **“high protein”**, **“low fat”** or other nutrition and health claims are subject to rules, but a valid claim still describes only one aspect of the product.

For example, a high-protein snack may also be high in salt or added sugars. A low-fat product may not automatically be the better choice for what you need. Turn the pack over and compare the full label.

## Portion size: useful, but check whose portion it is

Manufacturers may show nutrition per serving as well as per 100g. The serving figure is useful only if it resembles the amount you will actually eat.

If two similar products use different serving sizes, use the **per-100g figures** for the cleaner comparison.

## A practical decision hierarchy

When choosing between similar packaged foods, ask:

1. **What job is this food doing?** A main meal, protein source, snack, convenience option or treat?
2. **What are the main ingredients?** Do they make sense for that job?
3. **Which nutrition figures matter here?** Do not optimise every number simultaneously.
4. **Will I actually eat and enjoy it?** A theoretically perfect food that sits unused in the cupboard is not useful.
5. **Does the convenience justify the cost?** Convenience can be valuable if it helps a good routine survive busy days.

## What not to do

- Do not judge a whole diet from one ingredient you recognise as “bad”.
- Do not assume a long ingredient list automatically makes a product unhealthy.
- Do not assume “natural”, “organic” or premium-looking packaging tells you the nutritional profile.
- Do not compare unrelated foods as if they should have the same label numbers.
- Do not let label reading become a reason to fear normal food.

## A simple weekly experiment

Pick one category you buy often — cereal, yoghurt, bread, ready meals or snacks — and compare two or three options using the same criteria. Once you know your preferred option, stop analysing it every week.

The goal is **less decision fatigue**, not more.
